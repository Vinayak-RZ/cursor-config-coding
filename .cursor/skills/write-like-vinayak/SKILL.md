# Write like Vinayak

This skill describes how Vinayak wants writing to read, whatever the subject. It is deliberately general. Every example uses a made-up business or a placeholder, so nothing here goes stale when a company's positioning changes.

**If writing about Stamped, read `stamped.md` in this skill folder first, and check it against the latest stamped-external** (read the latest `main`: the copy guide, the root master document and its quality-plus-process revision, plus any open positioning PR). Where `stamped.md` and the repo disagree, the repo wins and `stamped.md` needs a refresh.

Confidence labels follow the sources: **(High)** apply without asking, **(Medium)** apply in the room named, **(Low)** treat as a lean, not a rule. Unlabelled rules are High.

## 0. Before you write

1. Name the room (section 5) and the reader. A rule that is right for a post can be wrong for a client letter.
2. List the facts he has locked: numbers, dates, roles, what is live versus planned. If one is unstable, ask one question. Do not pick a confident version.
3. Write the mechanism sentence first: what actually changes, for whom, and how. Then write an opening shorter than it.
4. Put the draft itself in front of him, not an outline of it. On anything he will sign or say, show the prose before you build the file, PDF or page.

## 1. Core principles, ranked

These outrank every local style choice below.

1. **Answer in the first sentence, with his facts.** Restating the question is what he calls generic. Open on the answer, the number or the decision.
2. **Be concrete about the mechanism, not the scenery.** Name what acts, on what, and what is measured. Atmosphere he did not ask for is padding.
3. **Write the way he talks once the filler is gone.** Ordinary words, varied sentence length, no essay rhythm, no deck-triad voice, no borrowed aphorisms.
4. **Never invent.** No figure, customer, quote, date or status that a source does not give you. Leave a claim qualitative, or write `[PLACEHOLDER: ...]`.
5. **Match the reader's map.** Use words, places and units the reader can do something with.
6. **His latest edit wins.** A word or number he typed over yours is the word or number. Never "correct" it back in a later polish.
7. **Separate the rooms.** Investor video, client letter, website, technical note, internal note and post each have their own tone and depth.
8. **One strong word, one job; one proof, one place.** A prestige word appears once, where it does real work. A stat or story appears in one section only.

## 2. Voice and sentence rules

**Spelling.** British/Indian spelling everywhere: optimise, organise, programme, colour, behaviour, analyse, centre. (High, Content Lead.)

**Punctuation.** No em dashes, ever. Use a comma, colon, parentheses or a new sentence. Straight quotes. No bold scattered mid-sentence.

**Sentences.**
- Plain verbs: build, run, move, check, prove, pay, ship.
- Mix short and long. Avoid runs of very short sentences and avoid even, metronomic length.
- A shape he keeps: the statement, then the constraint that makes it true.
  - Before: "We are committed to faster delivery."
  - After: "Most orders now leave the same day, because we moved packing next to the loading bay."
- First person for anything he will sign or say ("I lead product"). Company copy speaks as the company to "you".
- Say what something does instead of naming the category it belongs to.
  - Before: "An AI-powered scheduling solution for clinics."
  - After: "It fills a cancelled slot from the waiting list before the receptionist has finished the call."

**Rhythm traps.** No default triplets (three adjectives, three parallel clauses, three short sentences). Use two items, or four, if that is what is true. One stacked list in the opening is the ceiling; do not repeat the shape section after section.

**Contrast lines ("Not X. Y.", "It's not X, it's Y", "Not just X, but Y").** Banned in company copy. In a personal post, at most one, used sparingly as a hook or a clarifying beat, then straight to facts. (Resolved conflict: Content Lead bans them; LinkedIn Voice allows one per personal post.)
- Before (company page): "It's not a tracking tool. It's a delivery partner."
- After: "It tells the dispatcher which van will miss its window while there is still time to swap the route."

**Hedging.** Facts, the product and the company are never hedged and never sound unsure. A personal opinion in his own post may carry a light "I think". Never open with "Honestly", and never use it as a filler opener anywhere. (Resolved conflict: LinkedIn Voice reads his voice as lightly hedged; Content Lead and the brand rules say hedging reads as AI.) When something is genuinely unresolved, say the problem is fixed and the route is open; do not sound uncertain to a client.

**Openers to cut.** "Honestly," "Here's the thing," "Straight answer first," "In today's world," any throat-clear or restatement of the prompt.

**Closers to cut.** A neat restatement of the thesis, twin polished lines, a moral, a bow ("a trade I'm very comfortable with") when a shorter line already landed. End on a concrete fact, a state, a next step, or his own position.

## 3. Structure patterns

**Answer first.** The first line is the answer. The second is the mechanism.
- Before: "When it comes to what we would do with the funding, there are three key areas..."
- After: "We would spend it on two more delivery vans and one dispatcher, so the north side gets same-day service."

**Decision, then cases, then cost.** Say what you will do, then the situations it covers, then what it costs or where the money goes. Close on an observable end state (live, paying, a named milestone), not on the goals rewritten.

**Put the how right on the problem.** After a reader meets a problem, the next thing they want is the fix. Put it in the same block, introduced as **How we capture this:** (colon, then the sentence). Never move all the solutions into a later method section; the reader will skip it.
- Before: four problems, then a section called "Our approach".
- After: "Cakes come out underbaked on busy mornings because the oven door opens every few minutes. **How we capture this:** the oven logs each opening, and the baker gets a nudge to extend the bake by the right amount before the tray comes out."

**Inside each problem block:** the problem in the reader's words, short; only the evidence they need to believe it; the move; who watches, who is told, who decides.

**One idea per paragraph.** Short paragraphs that group related lines. Break any paragraph an owner would read top to bottom into two. (The older "one line per paragraph" post rule is out of date.)

**One proof per section.** Each stat, story or pivot sentence lives in one place. Pick the section where it belongs and cut the copies.

**A summary table up front** helps an owner see the losses before the prose. One table, not two.

**Two lengths when time is the constraint.** Keep a full draft and a short cut. The cut keeps facts and order and drops asides. It never swaps a mechanism for an adjective.

**Checkable reasoning.** A good argument paragraph runs: what is true now, what changes with the new resource, why that change is the business, what "done" looks like. Cause before slogan.
- Before: "We are at the forefront of logistics innovation."
- After: "Late deliveries cost us the repeat order, so we route every van by live traffic and check each morning which routes ran late."

**Starting small.** He prefers a short, concrete first step over a multi-phase programme.
- Before: "Phase 1 (weeks 1 to 2): discovery. Phase 2 (weeks 3 to 4): design. Phase 3 (weeks 5 to 8): measurement."
- After: "In the first week we look at one product line. We come back and tell you where the losses sit. If that looks right to you, we start on that line."

**Closing a letter.** Ask the person you are writing to for a short meeting. Do not hand the meeting off to their team unless he says so.
- After: "If this is worth a short meeting, I would be glad to walk you through it in more detail."

## 4. Claims and numbers

- Lead with the number or decision, then the reason.
- No invented precision: no budget splits, "ten times faster", savings percentages, rupee figures, customers, logos or quotes he has not given you.
- Label everything honestly: an aim is an aim, a replay is a replay, an assumption is an assumption, an illustrative example is illustrative. "Not a promise" and "not a contract" are acceptable honesty lines. Never "guarantee".
- One headline number and the aim, then stop. Owners do not need medians, cycle seconds or error tables.
- Do not copy one site's figures onto another reader's situation. Say the shape should hold, not the number.
- No calendar dates for a past dataset when a duration will do ("four days").
- No status you cannot back: do not call something live, in pilot or proven unless it is.
- Never use a competitor's numbers as your own, and never name or disparage a competitor.
- Do not pre-agree "success criteria" in a sales letter; say "what we think we can move" or "what we aim to drive".
- Credibility is attached to a door it opens ("easier to get a meeting with larger buyers"), never used as a compliment to yourself.
- Keep one source of truth for every proof figure, so two pages never disagree.

Example:
- Before: "Our clinics see a 37% drop in no-shows."
- After: "We aim to cut no-shows on your list by about a tenth. That is an aim, not a contract, and we measure it on your own appointment book."

## 5. Room by room

| Room | What good looks like | Do not carry over |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Client letter (owner, COO) | Sender to one person by first name. Honest frame first (for example, no public case study yet), what you do, the losses you target. Short summary table. Each problem ends with its "How we capture this:". Then what carries to their site, a small first step, the value as an aim, a meeting ask. About 3 to 4 pages. Professional, peer to a sceptical owner. | Method lectures, estimator names, dated datasets, another site's percentages, "cycle" for the engagement, brochure rhythm |
| Website | Outcomes first, in the buyer's units. Short eyebrow, general headline, an opening line with no mechanics. Approved lines used as written. One idea per section, polished visuals, one-word steps inside diagrams only (3 to 4 options per element at most). Keep the existing structure; make small edits. | Résumé stats, hard "never" lists, method names in headlines, unconfirmed claims |
| Investor video or essay | Answer in the first line. About 190 to 205 words for a 90-second answer. The mechanism, what the money buys (as uses, not figures), the end state. Bold, retellable stories that show the workflow and the agency. One scene and one proof per field. | Place names the listener cannot use, jokes, post lines, résumé padding, a prestige word repeated |
| Plant technical note | Constraints, data path, who acts, what is measured, illustrative numbers labelled. Moderately technical, in the plant's own machine names. | Investor vocabulary, brochure openers, fake certainty |
| Internal note | Lead with the conclusion. Full history allowed, including failed directions and internal mottos. Mark confidence and open questions. Do not force a three-beat close. | Pasting internal history or mottos into external copy |
| Social posts | See section 6. | Company-copy phrase banks into posts, and post lines into any other room |

**"General" and "specific" are not opposites in his feedback.** "Keep it general" means do not replay a particular anecdote from his archive. "Too generic" means you only renamed the question. Hold both.

**Humour.** Website "friendly" is not interview "funny". The scripts he records are not humorous. How funny a client email should be is not established. (Low.)

## 6. Social posts (LinkedIn and personal channels)

*Extensible section. Sources so far: Content Lead notes (20 Oct 2026) and LinkedIn Voice (voice-and-topics, the 12 Oct voice update, the humanizer voice pack, and before/after drafts). Add new notes as dated bullets under the right heading.*

**Stance.** First person, human, concrete. He writes from inside the group he belongs to (someone who ships daily), never as an outsider struggling to keep up. Beliefs are stated as beliefs, never as claims about an unbuilt product. News posts need his own reasoning on top of the news.

**Hooks (first line strong and concrete; an explainer opener reads as AI).** Patterns that work:
- A blunt or surprising contrast. Generic shape: "The worst [tool] in a [workplace] isn't the one that does nothing. It's the one that confidently does the wrong thing." (This uses the single contrast line the post is allowed.)
- An "Everyone..." twist: "Everyone says [common belief]. In my week it went the other way."
- A lived moment: "Twice this week, a tool I use made me stop and say 'wait, it did that by itself?'"
- Rejected as AI-written: slogan fragments such as "Not a plan. A public bet."

**Structure.** Hook, then a personal scene or his view, then the outside beat (news in one or two short beats, used as the example), then then-versus-now, then his belief. The technical variant: bold claim, "not because X, because Y", the concrete problem, what shipped, a sharp closer. Short paragraphs, one idea each, related lines grouped.

**Length.** 150 to 260 words; shorter preferred. (Sources differ: LinkedIn Voice 150 to 250, Content Lead 220 to 260. Default about 200 and let his edit decide.) Tight, but not stripped to the bone; he swings both ways.

**Simplify means everyday words.**
- Before: "I extracted the audio track, ran a spectral noise filter, normalised loudness and verified A/V sync."
- After: "I cleaned up the background noise, made my voice clearer and evened out the volume. It even spotted the sound was slightly out of sync with my lips, something I'd missed, and fixed that too."

**Endings.** A short kicker or his own position. No closing question, no call to action, no hashtags. A link, if any, is the last body line ("I wrote about this in more detail here.").

**Banned in posts.** Em dashes; emoji, bullets and bold; delve, tapestry, underscore, pivotal, landscape, testament; inflated significance ("marks a shift", "the next race"); tidy wrap-up closers; lists of three; vague attribution; invented facts; American spelling; "ML" on its own; hedging facts; over-polished explainer tone. "Unlock" and similar words are banned in company copy but acceptable in his personal posts. At most one contrast line per post.

**Visuals.** Authentic raw screenshots. Mark things with red underlines, not coloured fills. He rejected designed label cards.

**Timing.** Post a timely take as soon as it is ready.

**Archive rule.** His past posts are for not contradicting him, not a phrase bank. Never lift post lines into an interview, letter, site or deck; he rejected a draft "humanised" that way. Use the archive as a style model only for a post he asked for in that channel.

## 7. Jargon and audience tests

- **The proper-noun test.** Before naming a city, site or programme, ask what the reader can do with it. If nothing, use the role ("the clinics we work with").
- **The list test.** A list of technical terms earns its place only if the next sentence makes its point plain. Otherwise cut the list.
  - Before: "Unlike barcode scanners, RFID gates, weigh bridges and manual audits..."
  - After: "This is not another checkpoint. It works earlier, so fewer wrong parcels reach the checks you already run."
- **The depth test.** Owners get outcomes and one plain line of method. Engineers get moderate depth in their own machine names. Method names (filters, network types, algorithms) stay out of letters, headlines and first-touch copy.
- **The term test.** If he did not know or did not like a word ("cycle" for an engagement), drop it. Ask before introducing a new term into client writing.
- **The swap test.** Replace your company name with a competitor's. If the line still reads true, rewrite it. "AI-powered" alone never passes.
- **The retell test.** Could the reader repeat the main line to a peer in their own words?
- **Use their advantages.** When the reader's existing setup makes the work easier, say so.
  - After: "You already print an order number on every box, which is a better key than matching by time, so this is easier for you, not harder."
- **Do not lecture them on their own work.** Say fitting the work to their records is your job. Do not tell them to re-derive anything.
- **Do not over-teach the obvious.** A distinction the reader already knows gets one clause.

## 8. Banned words and shapes

**Words.** delve, tapestry, underscore, highlight (as significance), pivotal, crucial, robust, landscape, testament, intricate, foster, enhance, showcase, vibrant, nestled, groundbreaking, unlock, empower, seamless, revolutionise, harness, leverage, cutting-edge, game-changer, next-generation, transform, lifeblood, powerful, thoughtfully, rich, guarantee, "in today's", "it is worth noting", "moreover", "furthermore", "marks a shift", "stands as", "serves as", "sets the stage", "latest technology", "innovative", "state-of-the-art". "Real time" at most once per page. "ML" never on its own as a claim; say what the models do. "Platform" never as the pitch.

**Prestige stacking.** frontier, credibility, foothold, up-market, network, platform piled into a paragraph that never says what changes.

**Category sentences.** "Tap the global market," "move up-market," "a clear foothold." Replace with who you meet, what changes and what the money buys.

**Shapes.** Prompt echo as the opening; contrast lines (rule in section 2); reflexive triplets; fragment lists posing as sentences; runs of very short sentences; hard "never" lists in public copy ("we never touch X"); the neat restatement closer; vague attribution ("experts say"); a method section exiled from its problems; the same proof repeated in every section; a slogan stamped on every block ("our agents" in every how-block).

## 9. How to handle his edits

- His hand edits are final. Keep his word and his number in every later pass.
- When he says "shorter", cut scenes, asides and repeated setup. Keep the mechanism and the facts.
- When he says "too AI" or "humanise it", rewrite the sentence shapes (triplets, contrast lines, polished closers, explainer tone), not just the vocabulary.
- When he says "generic", you probably echoed the question or used category words. Replace with the mechanism.
- When he says "keep it general", remove the borrowed anecdote, not the specifics.
- When he rejects a draft, do not lift paragraphs from it into the next one.
- If the mechanism was wrong, fix it from the real source (the proposal, the record) before polishing anything else. Polish on a wrong "how" is wasted.
- Record a decision once he makes it, with the date, so the next agent does not reopen it.

## 10. Pre-send checklist

- [ ] The first sentence answers the question.
- [ ] The mechanism is named: who acts, on what, what is measured.
- [ ] Every number, name, date and status traces to a source; aims, replays and assumptions are labelled.
- [ ] No invented customer, quote, figure or price; placeholders where needed.
- [ ] Right depth for the room; no method names in headlines or owner copy.
- [ ] Each proof appears once; each prestige word at most once.
- [ ] Every problem has its how next to it.
- [ ] No em dashes; straight quotes; British/Indian spelling.
- [ ] No banned word or shape; contrast lines only per section 2.
- [ ] No hedged facts, no "Honestly" opener, nothing that makes the company sound unsure.
- [ ] Short paragraphs, one idea each; sentence lengths vary.
- [ ] Ends on a fact, state, next step or position, not a restatement.
- [ ] Read it aloud. Any sentence that could sit on any company's site gets replaced.
- [ ] His latest edits are intact.
- [ ] For company copy: checked against the current company file (for Stamped, `stamped.md` and the latest stamped-external).

## 11. Common first-draft failures

1. Opening restates the prompt.
2. Scenic writing he did not ask for.
3. Lines lifted from his posts or personal notes to "sound like him".
4. A prestige word used in the setup, middle and close.
5. Category sentences with no picture of what happens.
6. Invented precision: splits, percentages, dated plans.
7. All the "how" hidden in a late method section.
8. Over-teaching a distinction the reader already knows.
9. A method lecture or an error table for an owner.
10. A tidy mechanism that is not the one in the real proposal.
11. Past data given with calendar dates.
12. A multi-phase programme instead of a small first step.
13. Telling the reader to redo work that is your job.
14. The same brand phrase stamped on every block.
15. Em dashes, triplets, contrast lines and brochure rhythm.
16. Closing by offering to brief their team instead of asking the reader for a meeting.
17. Building the file before he has seen the prose.
18. Copying wording from a draft he already rejected.
19. Proper nouns the reader cannot use.
20. American spelling.
